# 2. Overview

## Purpose

What makes a website well designed?

At first, this may seem like a simple question. You may immediately recognize websites that appear polished, outdated, confusing, attractive, easy to use, or difficult to navigate.

Professional web design requires you to move beyond:

**“I like it.”**

or:

**“I don't like it.”**

A designer needs to explain **why** an interface works, **why** a user may experience difficulty, and **what evidence supports a recommended change**.

In this workshop, you will examine a real website, make initial design judgments, discuss those judgments with other students, and develop a structured critique based on the web-design foundations introduced in Topic 1.

The workshop follows the learning process:

**Observe → Judge → Explain → Discuss → Analyze → Recommend → Reflect**

# 7. Concepts & Theory

Following the prior-knowledge activity, the class will examine a starting model for effective web design:

**Good Web Design = Purpose + Audience + Content + Usability + Accessibility + Visual Communication + Technical Execution**

These areas are interconnected.

A visually attractive website can still fail if users cannot accomplish their goals.

A technically correct website can still communicate poorly.

A usable website can still exclude users if accessibility has not been considered.

Good web design requires these concerns to work together.

---

## Purpose

Every website exists for a reason.

Ask:

**What is this website trying to accomplish?**

A website may need users to:

* learn something;
* purchase something;
* register;
* contact an organization;
* find information;
* access a service; or
* complete another task.

---

## Audience

Websites are designed for people.

Ask:

**Who is expected to use this website?**

Consider how audience influences:

* language;
* content;
* navigation;
* typography;
* imagery;
* complexity;
* interaction; and
* accessibility.

---

## Content

Users usually visit websites because they want information or need to accomplish something.

Ask:

**Can users find and understand the information they need?**

Consider whether content is:

* relevant;
* understandable;
* organized;
* scannable; and
* appropriately prioritized.

---

## Usability

Usability considers whether people can effectively use an interface.

Ask:

**Can users accomplish their goals without unnecessary confusion or effort?**

Users should generally understand:

* where they are;
* what they can do;
* where they can go;
* what is interactive;
* what information is important; and
* whether an action has succeeded.

In Topic 2, you will develop this analysis further using **Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics**.

---

## Accessibility

Accessibility considers whether people with different abilities, technologies, and interaction methods can use a website.

You may notice issues involving:

* colour contrast;
* text size;
* link identification;
* keyboard interaction;
* headings;
* images;
* forms; or
* readability.

At this stage, you are making **initial observations**, not conducting a complete accessibility audit.

---

## Visual Communication

Visual design communicates information.

Elements such as:

* hierarchy;
* colour;
* typography;
* spacing;
* alignment;
* imagery;
* grouping; and
* consistency

influence what users notice and how they interpret an interface.

Ask:

**Does the visual presentation help users understand the content and available actions?**

---

## Technical Execution

A design must eventually function as a real website.

Technical execution can influence:

* responsive behaviour;
* navigation;
* interaction;
* readability;
* reliability; and
* the overall user experience.

Ask:

**Does the implemented website support or undermine the intended design?**

# 8. From Opinion to Design Critique

The instructor will demonstrate how to move from a personal reaction to a useful design observation.

### Personal Reaction

> “The page looks cluttered.”

This communicates a reaction but provides little evidence.

### Observation

> “Several headings, promotional elements, buttons, and images compete for attention near the top of the page.”

Now something specific has been identified.

### Interpretation

The competing elements make it difficult for the user to determine which information or action is most important.

### Recommendation

Reduce competing elements and create greater visual emphasis around the primary content and action.

The critique process becomes:

**Observation → Evidence → Interpretation → Recommendation**

You will use this process throughout the workshop and in your Student Submission Template.

# 11. Workshop Task 3 — First-Pass Observation

## What

Explore the website without immediately trying to solve its problems.

## Why

Good critique begins with careful observation.

If you jump immediately to solutions, you may recommend changes without fully understanding the problem.

## How

Explore:

* the home page;
* primary navigation;
* at least two additional pages;
* prominent calls-to-action;
* content organization;
* important interactive elements; and
* responsive behaviour where possible.

Record what you actually notice.

For example:

**Observation:** The home page contains three large promotional areas before the main service information.

is more useful at this stage than:

**Opinion:** The home page is bad.

# 12. Workshop Task 4 — Analyze Purpose, Audience & Content

## What

Investigate how effectively the website serves its intended purpose and audience.

## Why

Design cannot be evaluated meaningfully without understanding what it is trying to accomplish.

## How

Consider:

### Purpose

* What is the website trying to accomplish?
* Is that purpose obvious?
* What evidence supports your conclusion?

### Audience

* Who appears to be the intended audience?
* What content or design decisions suggest this?
* Is the interface appropriate for those users?

### Content

* What information appears most important?
* Does the design communicate that importance?
* Can users locate important information efficiently?

# 13. Workshop Task 5 — Usability Investigation

## What

## Why

A website may appear attractive but become difficult to use when someone actually tries to accomplish something.

## How

* finding contact information;
* locating a service;
* finding pricing;
* registering;
* finding a specific piece of information;
* navigating to a particular resource; or

For example:

**Home → Services → Web Services → Pricing → Goal**

Pay attention to:

* navigation labels;
* interaction;
* page hierarchy;
* feedback;
* uncertainty;
* unnecessary steps;
* confusing content; and
* successful design decisions.

# 18. Workshop Task 10 — Peer Design Critique

Exchange your analysis with another student or group.

The purpose is not to determine whose critique is “right.”

Instead, examine the **quality of the reasoning**.

Ask:

* Is the observation specific?
* Is evidence provided?
* Does the interpretation follow from the evidence?
* Is the recommendation connected to the problem?
* Is the recommendation based on user needs or personal preference?
* Has something important been overlooked?

Useful peer-critique questions include:

**What evidence supports that?**

**Would every user experience it that way?**

**Who might experience this differently?**

**Could your proposed change introduce another problem?**

**Does the recommendation actually address the issue you identified?**

# 22. Workshop Evaluation

**Workshop 1 — Website Design Critique — 3%**

The workshop evaluates your ability to participate in a structured design critique and communicate evidence-supported observations and recommendations.

Evaluation considers:

| Area                | Evidence                                                                                      |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Analysis**        | Purpose, audience, content, usability, visual communication, and accessibility are considered |
| **Evidence**        | Design judgments are supported by specific observations and appropriate screenshots           |
| **Reasoning**       | Observations are connected to likely effects on users                                         |
| **Recommendations** | Improvements respond logically to identified concerns                                         |
| **Participation**   | Student contributes to analysis, discussion, and peer critique                                |
| **Reflection**      | Individual reflection demonstrates thoughtful consideration of learning                       |

The objective is not to discover a predetermined list of “correct” problems.

The objective is to demonstrate a thoughtful process:

**Observe → Evidence → Interpret → Recommend**
